A well-trained dog is not a dog that obeys every command instantly. It is a dog that understands what is expected of it, feels safe and can therefore enjoy more freedom: walks in town, café terraces, visits to friends. The good news is that modern training is gentle, fun and within everyone’s reach. Here are the basics for a great start, with a puppy or an adult dog.

## The principles of kind training

### Reward what goes right

**Positive reinforcement** means immediately rewarding a behaviour you want to see again: a treat, a game, a stroke, a cheerful word. The dog quickly learns that the behaviour “pays”. Research in animal behaviour shows that this method works and keeps the relationship built on trust.

### Avoid physical punishment and intimidation

Shouting, shaking, hitting or using collars that hurt creates fear and stress, can make problems worse and may even trigger aggression. In Wallonia, **electric and choke collars are banned**, with very limited exceptions (police and army dogs, a choke collar on a vet’s certificate); a half-check collar with a stopper is still allowed. Flanders is banning electric-impulse collars from 1 January 2027, except collars linked to an invisible fence. The message is clear: we train without pain.

### Be consistent and patient

Everyone in the family uses the same words and the same rules. Sessions are short (5 minutes for a puppy, a little longer for an adult), several times a day, and always end on a success.

> **Good to know**: the right moment to reward is within a second of the behaviour. Some owners use a “marker”, a short word such as “yes!” or a clicker, to show the dog exactly what earned the reward.

## The essential skills

### Its name

Say its name in a happy voice and reward as soon as it looks at you. Its name should always announce something pleasant, never a telling-off.

### Sit and down

Hold a treat close to its nose and guide its head upwards: its bottom will naturally go down. Reward. For “down”, slowly lower the treat between its front paws. Add the word once the movement is fluent.

### Recall

This is the most important skill for your dog’s safety.

1. Start at home, with no distractions, a few metres away.
2. Call with a single cheerful word (“here!”), crouch down and reward generously when it arrives.
3. Increase the difficulty very gradually: garden, then a quiet park with a 5 to 10-metre long line.
4. Never call your dog for something unpleasant (end of the walk, a bath, a telling-off).

### Loose-lead walking

A puppy does not know how to walk on a lead: it has to be taught. Reward every time the lead is slack beside you. If it pulls, stop and wait for it to come back towards you before moving on. A well-fitted harness protects its throat while it learns.

### “Leave it” and “give”

Learning to let go of an object in exchange for a treat can prevent a dangerous item being swallowed. Practise first with toys, and often give the object back: your dog learns that giving does not mean losing.

### Being alone

Get your dog used to short absences from the day it arrives, and build them up gradually. Leave it a chew or a food-stuffed toy. Calm departures and returns, without long goodbyes or over-excited greetings, help it cope with being alone.

## Socialisation: a precious window

Between about 3 and 12 to 14 weeks, a puppy goes through a **socialisation period** during which it learns very easily that the world is safe. What it discovers positively at this age, it will accept much more easily as an adult.

### Things worth introducing

- **All kinds of people**: children, older people, people wearing hats, glasses or hoods, on bikes or in wheelchairs.
- **Other animals**: calm, vaccinated adult dogs, cats, horses at a distance.
- **Places**: towns, public transport, markets, the car, the countryside.
- **Sounds**: vacuum cleaner, doorbell, traffic, thunder (at low volume at first).
- **Handling**: touching paws, ears and mouth, brushing, being examined at the vet’s.

### The golden rules

- Always at its own pace: if it is scared, increase the distance and never force it.
- Short, positive experiences paired with treats or play.
- Quality over quantity: one pleasant encounter is worth more than ten stressful ones.

Outings are possible before the vaccination course is complete, as long as you avoid high-risk places (other dogs’ droppings, areas busy with unknown dogs). Ask your vet for advice.

## Puppy classes and dog trainers

A **puppy class** run by a dog trainer offers a safe setting to play with other puppies and learn the basics. For adult dogs, group or one-to-one lessons are just as useful.

### Choosing a trainer

Dog training is not a regulated profession in Belgium: anyone can call themselves a trainer. A few criteria will help you choose:

- They use and explain **positive methods**, with no shock, choke or prong collars.
- They keep their training up to date and are happy to tell you about it.
- They let you watch a class before you sign up.
- Groups are small and dogs are never forced.
- They refer you to a vet or behaviourist when a problem goes beyond training.

> **Good to know**: a dog that pulls, jumps up or barks is not being “dominant”. Most of the time it simply has not learned another behaviour yet, or it is showing excitement or worry.

## In short

Reward, repeat, stay consistent and patient: basic training rests on simple habits. Combined with gentle socialisation in the first months, it lays the foundations for a well-balanced dog and a lifelong relationship of trust.
