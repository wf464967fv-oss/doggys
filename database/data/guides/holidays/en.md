The holidays are coming and the big question arises: is your dog coming with you, or staying in Belgium in good hands? Both options can work beautifully. The key is to prepare in time, because some steps take several weeks. Here is your guide to setting off with peace of mind.

## Should your dog come along?

Ask yourself a few honest questions:

- **The climate**: flat-faced, older or thick-coated dogs cope badly with heatwaves.
- **The type of holiday**: mountain hiking, yes; days spent in museums and on dog-free beaches, much less so.
- **The journey**: long hours in the car or a flight in the hold can be tough.
- **Its temperament**: some dogs love new things, others are happiest with their usual routine.

If the trip is likely to be an ordeal for your dog, a good care arrangement is often the kindest choice.

## Travelling in the EU with your dog

To cross a border within the EU, three conditions are essential:

1. **Identification by microchip** (or a legible tattoo applied before 3 July 2011).
2. **The European pet passport**, issued by your vet. In Belgium, every identified dog receives one.
3. **A valid rabies vaccination**, recorded in the passport.

### Rabies vaccine timing

- The first vaccine can be given from **12 weeks** of age.
- It only becomes valid **21 days after the injection**, so a puppy cannot travel before **15 weeks**.
- A booster given before the expiry date is valid immediately. If it is given later, you start again as with a first vaccination, with a new 21-day wait.

> **Good to know**: rabies vaccination is no longer compulsory for a dog that stays in Belgium, but it is required for travel. Check the validity date in the passport at least a month before you leave.

### Countries requiring tapeworm treatment

To enter **Ireland, Northern Ireland, Finland, Norway or Malta**, your dog must be treated against the tapeworm *Echinococcus multilocularis* by a vet **between 24 and 120 hours (1 to 5 days) before arrival**, with the treatment recorded in the passport. Great Britain requires a similar treatment.

### Outside the EU

Each country sets its own conditions: health certificate, deadlines, sometimes quarantine or restrictions on certain breeds. Check with the destination country’s embassy several months ahead. On your return, depending on the country visited, a **rabies antibody test** carried out well before departure may be required to re-enter the EU. Discuss it with your vet early on.

### Other health precautions

In southern Europe, insect-borne diseases such as **leishmaniasis** (sandflies) or heartworm (mosquitoes) are more common than in Belgium. Your vet can recommend protection suited to your destination. Do not forget about ticks.

## A safe journey

### By car

- Secure your dog with a **harness attached to the seat belt** or in a **fixed crate**: in an emergency stop, a loose dog is thrown forward.
- Stop roughly every two hours for a drink and a leg stretch.
- **Never leave your dog alone in a car** in the sun, not even for a few minutes with the windows cracked: the temperature rises very quickly and heatstroke can be fatal.

### By train, ferry or plane

Each company has its own rules (size, crate, muzzle, extra fee). Book early, as the number of animals is often limited. On planes, the cabin is generally reserved for small dogs; travelling in the hold deserves careful discussion with your vet, especially for flat-faced breeds.

### At your destination

Find a vet near where you are staying, take your dog’s usual food, bowls, bed, a first-aid kit and an ID tag with your phone number. Check that your accommodation genuinely welcomes dogs.

## Leaving your dog in Belgium

### Boarding kennels

In Belgium, boarding kennels must be **licensed** by their region. Before booking:

- **Visit**: cleanliness, relaxation areas, outdoor runs, isolation of sick dogs.
- **Ask questions**: how often dogs go out, walks, contact between dogs, how vet emergencies are handled.
- **Check the required vaccinations**: most kennels ask for up-to-date core vaccines and often the **kennel cough** vaccine, to be given a few weeks before the stay.
- **Book early**: in summer and over the festive season, places go fast.

A short trial stay of a night or a weekend helps your dog get used to it before the big holiday.

### Pet sitters

A pet sitter visits your home once or several times a day, or stays in your home while you are away. Your dog keeps its familiar surroundings: a precious solution for older or anxious dogs and those who struggle in a group setting.

- Meet them beforehand, together with your dog.
- Leave written instructions: meals, walks, habits, medication, your vet’s details.
- Agree on the price, schedule and updates (photos, messages).
- Let your vet know that someone else may bring your dog in.

### Family or friends

A warm solution, as long as the person is available, comfortable with dogs and has a secure home.

> **Good to know**: whatever you choose, hand over the **European pet passport** or a copy, the health record and your vet’s details. Also check that your phone number is up to date in DogID.

## Pre-departure checklist

- **Two to three months before**: decide between travelling and care, book the kennel or pet sitter, check the destination’s requirements.
- **One month before**: check the rabies vaccination and any vaccines required by the kennel, restock flea and tick treatments.
- **The week you leave**: tapeworm treatment if needed, pack your dog’s bag, written instructions for the carer.
- **On the day**: a calm departure, without drawn-out goodbyes.
