The big day is nearly here: your puppy will soon discover its new home. For a puppy this is a huge change. It is leaving its mother, its littermates and every smell and sound it knows. A well-prepared arrival helps it gain confidence faster, and spares you quite a few sleepless nights. Here is how to organise things, step by step.

## The kit to buy before the big day

There is no need to empty the pet shop: the essentials fit on a short list.

- **A bed** sized for its adult build, washable, in a quiet spot but not cut off from the family.
- **Two stable bowls** for water and food, ideally stainless steel or ceramic.
- **The same food as at the breeder’s or shelter** for the first few days. Ask for the exact brand and daily amount.
- **A light collar or harness** and a 1.5 to 2-metre lead. A Y-shaped harness protects the throat of puppies that pull.
- **An ID tag** with your phone number, in addition to the microchip.
- **A variety of toys**: things to chew (essential while teething), to tug and to stuff with food.
- **A travel crate** for the car, which can also become a reassuring den at home if introduced gently.
- **Poo bags**, an enzymatic cleaner for little accidents and a brush suited to its coat.

> **Good to know**: avoid very small toys, cooked bones and ropes that fray. Puppies swallow anything they find, and swallowed objects are one of the most common emergencies.

## Puppy-proofing the house and garden

Get down on all fours and look at the room from a puppy’s height. You will quickly see what tempts it.

- **Electric cables**: hide them or cover them with cable trunking.
- **Cleaning products, medicines, cosmetics**: store them high up or in closed cupboards.
- **Toxic plants**: many house and garden plants can be harmful. Ask your vet or garden centre for advice.
- **Dangerous foods**: chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions, garlic and xylitol (found in some chewing gum and “sugar-free” products) must stay out of reach.
- **Stairs and balconies**: a gate prevents falls, especially for small breeds.
- **The garden**: check the fence, gaps under the hedge and access to any pool or pond.

## The first day: calm and routine

If you can, plan the arrival at the start of a weekend or a few days off.

### The journey home

Have someone sit next to the puppy or use a well-secured crate, with a towel, water and one or two breaks on a long journey. Ask the breeder or shelter for a piece of cloth carrying the scent of the mother or litter: a precious comfort for the first night.

### Arriving home

Start by showing your puppy where it should go to the toilet, then let it explore one or two rooms calmly. Avoid inviting the whole family and the neighbours on the first day: it needs peace and quiet. Children can join in, sitting on the floor and letting the puppy come to them.

### House rules

Agree as a family from the start on what is allowed: sofa or not, which rooms are open, and which words you will use for cues. A puppy learns much faster when everyone says the same thing.

## The first night (and the ones after)

It is normal for a puppy to cry during the first nights: it has never slept alone before. A few tips help a lot:

- Put its bed near your bedroom, or in your bedroom for the first few nights, then move it gradually if you wish.
- A last toilet trip just before bedtime limits night-time accidents.
- A young puppy often needs to go out once during the night. Keep it boring, with no play or excitement, then straight back to bed.
- A warm (not hot) water bottle wrapped in a cloth, or the cloth from the litter, can be reassuring.

## Toilet training: patience and routine

House-training usually takes a few weeks to a few months. The principle: create as many chances to succeed as possible, and reward them.

1. Take your puppy out when it wakes up, after every meal, after every play session and roughly every two hours.
2. Always go to the same spot and stay with it.
3. Praise it warmly and give a treat as soon as it has been outside.
4. If there is an accident indoors, clean up without scolding: punishment after the fact makes no sense to a puppy and may teach it to hide.
5. Use an enzymatic cleaner to remove smells that would draw it back to the same spot.

## The first important appointments

### At the vet’s

Book an appointment within a week of arrival. Your vet will check your puppy’s general health, its microchip and DogID registration, its vaccination record and worming schedule. It is also a good time to talk about food, flea and tick protection and neutering. Bring all the documents you were given.

### Registration in your name

Your puppy must already be microchipped and registered in DogID before it is handed over. Check that the change of keeper has been made: you should receive a registration certificate in your name. If in doubt, your vet can check.

### Socialisation

Between about 3 and 12 to 14 weeks, a puppy goes through a period when it learns very easily that the world is safe: noises, people, children, other vaccinated dogs, cars, towns. Offer short, positive discoveries and never force it. A puppy class run by a dog trainer is an excellent idea: ask your vet when your puppy can join, depending on its vaccinations.

> **Good to know**: puppies sleep a lot, up to 18 hours a day. Protect their naps, especially if there are children at home: an overtired puppy quickly becomes nippy and grumpy.

## In short

A successful arrival comes down to three words: preparation, calm and routine. Once the essential kit is bought, the house is safe, the rules are shared by the whole family and the first appointments are booked, all that is left is to enjoy these first weeks, which fly by. And if you have any doubts, your vet and a dog trainer are there to help.
