Does your dog bark the moment you leave, lunge at other dogs on the lead, tremble at the slightest noise, or has it already growled, or even bitten? You are not alone, and it is neither a failure nor a lost cause. Behaviour problems are common and, in the vast majority of cases, they improve with the right help. You just need to know who to turn to, and when.

## Trainer, behaviourist, vet: who does what?

These three professionals complement each other, but their roles differ.

### The dog trainer

They teach **skills**: recall, loose-lead walking, house-training, good manners. They help owners communicate with their dog. A trainer is the right person for a dog that simply has not yet learned what is expected of it.

### The behaviourist

They look at the **causes** of problem behaviour: fear, frustration, anxiety, bad experiences, an unsuitable environment. They observe the dog in context, look at its history and build a behaviour modification plan.

### The veterinary behaviourist

This is a **vet** with additional training in behavioural medicine. They can carry out a medical examination, look for a physical cause and, if necessary, **prescribe treatment**. For severe problems (serious aggression, disabling anxiety), they are often the best place to start.

> **Good to know**: in Belgium, the titles “dog trainer” and “behaviourist” are not protected. Only vets belong to a regulated profession. So ask about the training and methods of the person you consult.

## Start with your vet

A change in behaviour very often has a **medical cause**: joint pain, a dental problem, an ear infection, a hormonal disorder, failing sight or hearing, or cognitive decline in an older dog. A dog in pain can become irritable or bite when touched.

Before any behavioural work, especially if the change is recent or sudden, book an appointment with your vet. They will rule out a physical cause and can refer you on.

## When to seek help

Do not wait until the situation becomes unbearable: the earlier a problem is addressed, the easier it is to improve.

### Aggression

Growling, lifting the lips, snapping, biting people or other animals. **A bite, even a light one, always warrants professional help**, especially if there are children in the home. In the meantime, avoid trigger situations and do not punish growling: it is a valuable warning signal.

### Fears and phobias

Fear of noises (thunderstorms, fireworks, traffic), unfamiliar people, other dogs or new places. A dog that lives in fear is suffering, even if it is not “doing anything wrong”.

### Separation anxiety

Barking, howling, targeted destruction (doors, windows), house-soiling or heavy drooling **only when you are out**. Sometimes the neighbours notice before you do. Filming your dog when you leave is extremely helpful for the professional.

### Reactivity on the lead

The dog barks, pulls or lunges at other dogs, cyclists or joggers on walks. It is often driven by fear or frustration, rarely by “dominance”.

### Repetitive behaviours

Tail chasing, licking until a wound appears, compulsively staring at shadows or reflections.

### Difficulties after adoption

An adult dog that has not settled after several weeks, or tension between two dogs in the same home.

## What happens in a consultation?

### The interview

The first consultation often lasts one to two hours. The professional asks lots of questions: your dog’s history, a typical day, diet, activities, problem situations, what you have already tried. They observe your dog and the way you interact with it. It often takes place at home, where the problems actually happen.

### The action plan

You come away with an explanation of what your dog is feeling and a practical plan: changes to the environment, gradual exercises, managing risky situations, and sometimes extra activities. Recognised methods rely on **desensitisation**, **counter-conditioning** and **positive reinforcement**, never on pain or intimidation.

### Follow-up

Change takes time: a few weeks to several months. A good professional plans follow-up sessions, adjusts the plan and stays available for your questions.

> **Good to know**: your involvement is key. Progress depends above all on the exercises you practise every day. Be honest about what is realistic for your family: the plan should fit your life.

## Choosing the right professional

- They ask first whether your dog has had a **vet check**.
- They explain their methods and refuse aversive tools (shock, choke or prong collars). Electric and choke collars are already banned in Wallonia (with limited exceptions), and Flanders is banning electric-impulse collars from 1 January 2027, except for invisible fences.
- They do not promise miracle results in one session.
- They work with a vet and a trainer when needed.
- They treat you with respect and without judgement.

## What you can do right now

- **Keep a log** of when the problem happens: place, time, who was there, what happened just before.
- **Avoid** trigger situations as far as possible, so the problem is not reinforced.
- **Keep everyone safe**: lead, distance from other dogs, a gate for children. If your dog has already bitten, gradually teaching it to accept a well-fitted muzzle, with rewards, can be a useful safety measure.
- **Do not punish**: punishment increases fear and can make the warning signals disappear.

## In short

Seeing a behaviourist is not an admission of failure: it shows how much you care about your dog. Start with a vet check, choose a professional who uses kind methods and give yourself time. Most dogs make real progress, and family life becomes calmer again.
