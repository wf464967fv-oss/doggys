In the pet shop and on social media, dog food sparks heated debate. Grain-free kibble, gourmet wet food, raw feeding, home cooking… It is hard to know where to start. The good news: there is no single right answer, but several good ways to feed a dog, as long as you follow a few principles. Here is what you need to make an informed choice, to be fine-tuned with your vet.

## What a dog needs

A dog needs a **complete and balanced** diet: good-quality protein, fats, some carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals (with a proper calcium-to-phosphorus balance) and constant access to fresh water. Its needs depend on:

- **Age**: a growing puppy has different needs from an adult or a senior.
- **Adult size**: large-breed puppies must grow at a controlled pace to protect their joints.
- **Activity**: a sporting or working dog burns far more energy than a dog living in a flat.
- **Neutering**, which often lowers energy needs.
- **Health**: some conditions call for a special diet prescribed by the vet.

## Kibble: practical and balanced

Kibble is the most popular option. A dry food labelled “complete” is formulated to cover all a dog’s needs at a given life stage.

**Pros**: easy to store and measure, guaranteed balance with a good-quality food, generally affordable, handy when travelling or at the kennel.

**Cons**: quality varies hugely between brands, some dogs find it less tasty, and some dogs drink too little.

### Reading the label

- Look for the words **“complete feed”** (not “complementary”) and the life stage (puppy, adult, senior).
- Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight. A clearly named animal protein source near the top is a good sign.
- **Grain-free** is not automatically better: well-cooked grains are easily digested by most dogs. It only matters for some dogs, on veterinary advice.

## Wet food: tasty and full of moisture

Wet food (tins, trays, pouches) contains around 75 to 80% water.

**Pros**: very palatable, useful for fussy or older dogs and dogs that drink little.

**Cons**: more expensive for the same amount of energy, does not keep well once opened, and can encourage tartar when fed on its own.

Many families mix kibble and wet food. That works, provided you **reduce the amount of each** so the total does not exceed your dog’s needs.

## Home-cooked meals

Cooking for your dog with meat, starches and cooked vegetables can be a good solution, especially for dogs with intolerances. But a diet made up “by feel” is almost always unbalanced, particularly in calcium, vitamins and trace elements. If you are tempted, have a recipe calculated by a vet trained in nutrition and stick to it, mineral supplements included.

## Raw feeding (BARF): benefits and risks

BARF (*Biologically Appropriate Raw Food*) means feeding raw meat, raw meaty bones, offal and sometimes vegetables and fruit.

### Why people like it

Fans appreciate how much dogs enjoy it, the “natural” approach and the control over ingredients, and often notice a shiny coat and smaller stools.

### What you need to know

- **The risk of imbalance** is high if the proportions are not calculated. In puppies, a calcium error can cause growth problems.
- **Hygiene risks** are real: raw meat can carry bacteria (Salmonella, *E. coli*, *Listeria*…) that can make both the dog and the people in the household ill, especially young children, pregnant women, older people and anyone with a weakened immune system.
- **Bones** can fracture teeth or cause blockages or perforations in the gut. **Cooked bones** are strictly off-limits: they become brittle and splinter.

### If you choose raw feeding

Have a balanced diet drawn up by a professional with nutrition expertise, follow strict hygiene (separate chopping board and utensils, wash hands and bowls, respect the cold chain), and tell your vet, who will take the diet into account during check-ups and blood tests.

> **Good to know**: no option is “the best” for every dog. A healthy dog at its ideal weight, with a shiny coat and regular stools, is a well-fed dog, whatever feeding method you choose.

## How much to give?

The amounts on the packaging are a starting point. Adjust them by watching your dog.

- **The rib test**: you should feel the ribs easily under your hand without seeing them stick out. Seen from above, there should be a visible waist.
- **Regular weighing**: once a month, for example at the vet’s, or by weighing yourself with and without your dog in your arms.
- **Treats count**: they should not make up more than about 10% of daily intake. During training, set aside part of the daily ration as rewards.

Being overweight is one of the most common health problems in dogs. It increases the risk of arthritis and diabetes and shortens life expectancy.

## Changing food gently

Any change should be gradual, over about 7 to 10 days, mixing an increasing proportion of the new food into the old. A sudden switch often causes diarrhoea or vomiting. If the problems persist, ask your vet for advice.

## Dangerous foods

Some everyday foods are toxic to dogs, even in small amounts:

- **Chocolate**, especially dark and baking chocolate.
- **Grapes** and raisins.
- **Onions, garlic**, shallots and leeks, raw or cooked.
- **Xylitol**, a sweetener found in some chewing gum, sweets and “sugar-free” products.
- **Avocado**, macadamia nuts, alcohol and caffeine.
- **Cooked bones** and very fatty or salty leftovers.

If your dog has eaten any of these, contact your vet or an emergency vet straight away, without waiting for symptoms.

## In short

Kibble, wet food, home-cooked or raw: choose the option that suits your dog, your budget and your routine, then pay attention to balance, portions and hygiene. Your vet is your best ally for adapting your dog’s diet at every stage of its life.
