You have chosen a breed and would like a puppy from a breeder? A great idea, as long as you choose the breeder carefully. A responsible breeder selects healthy, well-balanced parents, socialises the puppies and supports you long after the sale. Puppy trafficking, on the other hand, thrives on online classifieds, with animals that are often sick and weaned too early. Here is how to tell the difference.

## What Belgian law says

Dog breeding and sales are regulated by the regions. The main rules:

- **The puppy must be at least 8 weeks old** when it is sold or given away.
- **It must be microchipped and registered in DogID before the transfer**, with a European pet passport.
- **The breeder must comply** with the rules of their region.

### In Wallonia

Every dog breeder must hold a **licence**, including for occasional breeding (up to two litters a year). Licence categories depend on the number of breeding females and litters. The **licence number must appear in adverts**. A buyer resident in Wallonia must show the extract from the central register obtained from their municipality (the *permis de détention*); a buyer living elsewhere needs no Walloon document.

### In Flanders

A breeder with **three or more litters a year** must be licensed and receives an **HK number**. Below that threshold, they are an occasional breeder, who must still follow strict rules. Only breeders may sell dogs, and adverts must show each puppy’s **microchip number**. A licensed breeder shows their **HK number** in every advert. An occasional breeder may only advertise in specialist media approved by the Flemish Animal Welfare department, never on general classified-ad sites or social media. A “Flemish” puppy offered on social media without an HK number is therefore a red flag. It is forbidden to give false information about age, origin or breed, to use misleading photos, to offer discounts or to sell an animal that is visibly ill.

### In Brussels

A licence from **Brussels Environment** is required from the “amateur” level upwards (at least two breeding females), as well as for boarding kennels, shelters and pet shops. A private person with fewer than three litters a year needs no licence, but may only advertise in specialist media.

> **Good to know**: a licence or HK number in an advert is a good start, not an absolute guarantee. If in doubt, check it with the regional authority, and trust what you see on site.

## The hallmarks of a good breeder

### They welcome you into their home

A responsible breeder invites you to visit, often several times before the puppy leaves. You see where the puppies live: a clean, warm, stimulating place, ideally part of everyday family life.

### They introduce you to the mother

The mother should be there, healthy and sociable. The father is not always on site (he may belong to another breeder), but you should be able to get his details.

### They work on the health of their breed

Depending on the breed, recommended screening may include hip and elbow scoring, eye tests, DNA tests or heart checks. A good breeder knows the weak points of their breed and shows you the parents’ results.

### They socialise their puppies

The first weeks are crucial. Puppies need to discover household noises, different surfaces and different people, and be handled gently. Ask how the breeder goes about it.

### They ask you questions

A breeder who cares about their puppies wants to know where they are going: your home, your routine, your experience. They may even turn you down if they feel the puppy would not suit your life. That is a good sign.

### They stay in touch after the sale

Advice on food, training and health: a good breeder answers your questions throughout the dog’s life and would rather take a dog back than see it abandoned.

## The documents you should receive

When you collect your puppy, you should receive:

- The **European pet passport**, with the microchip number and vaccinations.
- The **DogID registration certificate** or proof of transfer to your name.
- A written **sale contract**, with the breeder’s identity, licence number, a description of the puppy and the price.
- Information on worming and diet.
- For a pedigree dog, the **pedigree certificate**: in Belgium, it is issued by the recognised stud book (LOSH, run by the Royal Saint-Hubert Society). A “pedigree dog without papers” comes with no guarantee of breed at all.

## Warning signs of a scam

Puppy scams are common, especially online. Be on your guard if:

- **The price is unusually low** for the breed, or presented as a “special offer”.
- **Several breeds** are available at once, with puppies always ready to go.
- **A visit is impossible**: the seller offers delivery, or a meeting in a car park or petrol station.
- **The mother is “not there”** on the day of your visit.
- **The puppy is under 8 weeks old**, or has foreign paperwork while being described as born in Belgium.
- **You are asked for a deposit** before any contact, by transfer to a foreign account or through a money transfer service.
- **The photos** look too professional or appear in other adverts (a reverse image search helps check this).
- **The seller rushes you**: “other families are interested, you need to decide today”.

If in doubt, pay nothing and step back. A trafficked puppy often costs a fortune in vet bills, and brings a great deal of suffering for the puppy and for you.

> **Good to know**: an imported puppy must be at least 15 weeks old, because it cannot be vaccinated against rabies before 12 weeks and must then wait 21 days. A “9-week-old puppy from abroad” is therefore a major red flag.

## After the purchase

Book a vet appointment within a few days. Your vet will check the puppy’s health, chip and registration, and advise you for the future. If you discover a significant health problem, contact the breeder promptly in writing: the sale of animals in Belgium is covered by legal guarantees, and a vet can document the situation.

## In short

Visit, meet the mother, check the licence and the paperwork, ask questions and never give in to pressure: these few rules rule out most nasty surprises. Taking the time to choose your breeder well is already taking care of your future dog.
