Nobody likes to think about it, but knowing how to recognise an emergency can save your dog’s life. In some situations, minutes count. In others, an appointment the next day is fine. This guide helps you tell the difference and keep a cool head. It never replaces a vet’s advice: **if in doubt, call**. No vet will ever blame you for phoning about nothing.

## Get prepared before you need to be

The best time to get organised is when everything is fine.

- **Save in your phone** your vet’s number and that of a **24/7 emergency veterinary clinic** near you. On Doggys you can find them by municipality.
- **Find out about your vet’s out-of-hours service**: outside opening hours, many practices redirect callers to a duty vet via their answering machine.
- **Put together a small kit**: gauze pads, bandage, an animal-safe disinfectant, saline solution, a tick remover, a foil blanket, a well-fitting muzzle or a bandage to improvise one.
- **Note the key information**: your dog’s weight, current treatments, known allergies.

## Absolute emergencies: call immediately

In these situations, contact your vet or an emergency service **straight away**, and let them know you are on your way.

### Breathing difficulties

Very fast or noisy breathing, mouth wide open at rest, neck stretched out, bluish or very pale gums or tongue. A dog that cannot breathe normally is always an emergency.

### A swelling belly and unproductive retching

A belly that swells and hardens, together with retching that brings nothing up, restlessness and drooling, may signal **gastric dilatation-volvulus** (bloat with a twisted stomach). It mainly affects large, deep-chested dogs, often after a meal. It is a life-threatening surgical emergency.

### Collapse or a seizure

Loss of consciousness, sudden collapse, convulsions. During a seizure, move objects away from your dog, keep your hands away from its mouth and time how long it lasts. A seizure lasting more than a few minutes, or several seizures close together, needs immediate care. Even a single seizure warrants a call to your vet.

### It has swallowed something toxic

Chocolate, grapes, xylitol, human medicines (anti-inflammatories, paracetamol…), rat poison, antifreeze, toxic plants, household products. **Call right away**, even if there are no symptoms yet: some poisons only take effect hours or days later. Do not make your dog vomit yourself unless the vet specifically tells you to. Take the packaging or a photo of the product with you.

### Heatstroke

Intense panting, thick saliva, bright red gums, weakness, vomiting, unsteady walking. It can happen very quickly in a car in the sun, during exercise in warm weather or in flat-faced breeds.

- Move your dog into the shade or somewhere cool immediately.
- Wet it with **cool, not ice-cold** water, especially on the belly, armpits and legs, and fan it.
- Offer water to drink, without forcing it.
- **Head to the vet** while continuing to cool it, even if it seems to be recovering: complications can appear later.

### An accident or heavy bleeding

Hit by a car, a fall from height, a deep bite wound, bleeding that will not stop. Press a gauze pad or clean cloth on the wound, move your dog as little as possible (on a board or a blanket held taut) and go to the emergency vet. Even if it gets up and seems fine, internal injuries are possible.

### It cannot pass urine

A dog that squats or lifts its leg, strains and produces nothing or just a few drops may have a urinary blockage. This is an emergency.

### A difficult birth

Strong contractions for more than 30 minutes with no puppy born, a very lethargic mother, abnormal discharge: call your vet.

> **Good to know**: a dog in pain, even the gentlest one, may bite out of fear or pain. Handle it carefully and, if necessary, use a muzzle, unless it is vomiting or struggling to breathe.

## Situations to have checked quickly

They are not always life-threatening, but these signs warrant a call to the vet **the same day**:

- **Repeated vomiting or diarrhoea**, especially with blood, in a puppy, an older dog or a dog that seems flat.
- **Refusing food** for more than 24 hours, or refusing water.
- **Marked lameness**, refusing to put a paw down.
- **A painful eye**, closed, red or cloudy.
- **An insect sting** with significant swelling of the head or neck.
- **Contact with oak processionary caterpillars** (swollen tongue, drooling): rinse the mouth with water without rubbing and call.
- **Fever, unusual lethargy**, whimpering, a hunched posture.
- **A swallowed object** (sock, toy, bone, corn cob), even if your dog seems fine.

## The right reflexes in an emergency

1. **Stay calm**: your dog picks up on your stress.
2. **Call before you set off**: the vet can give you instructions and prepare for your arrival. Out of hours, listen to your practice’s answering machine or call a duty clinic directly.
3. **Give no human medicines**: many are toxic to dogs.
4. **Give no food or water** if your dog is vomiting, very lethargic or may need surgery, unless told otherwise.
5. **Transport it safely**: a small crate for small dogs, a taut blanket or board for large ones, one adult driving and another keeping watch.
6. **Bring** its passport, a list of its treatments and, in case of poisoning, the product packaging.

## Prevention is better than cure

- Keep medicines, household products and dangerous foods out of reach.
- Avoid stick games and cooked bones.
- Never leave your dog in a car in the sun, and adapt walks during hot weather.
- Split large dogs’ meals and avoid exercise straight after eating.
- Follow the preventive care your vet recommends: vaccinations, parasite protection, regular check-ups.

## In short

Laboured breathing, a swollen belly, collapse, seizures, poisoning, heatstroke, an accident, inability to urinate: in these situations every minute counts. Keep the right numbers to hand, stay calm and always call before setting off. When in doubt, one call too many is better than one call too late.
