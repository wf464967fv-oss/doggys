Every year, thousands of dogs wait for a family in Belgian shelters. Puppies, adults in their prime, seniors with hearts of gold: there is often a dog that fits your life perfectly. Adopting from a shelter means giving a second chance… and getting so much back. Here is how to make sure the story gets off to a good start.

## Why adopt from a shelter?

- **A known personality**: the staff watch every dog day in, day out. They know whether it likes children, tolerates cats, copes with being alone or loves long walks.
- **A dog that has been cared for**: as a rule it is chipped, registered, vaccinated, wormed and often neutered before adoption.
- **Ongoing support**: good shelters give advice, follow up after adoption and stay available if things get difficult.
- **A choice that matters**: every adoption frees up a space for another animal in need.

> **Good to know**: you will also find puppies and pedigree dogs in shelters. A shelter dog is not a “problem dog”: many end up there because of a house move, a death, a separation or an allergy in the family.

## Choosing a licensed shelter

In Belgium, animal shelters must be licensed by their region. The licence guarantees standards for housing, care and follow-up. Be cautious with “rescues” offering dogs imported from abroad with no chance to meet them first, handovers in car parks or no Belgian paperwork: the health risks (including rabies) and the disappointments are real.

Before you visit, look at the shelter’s website: most present their dogs with an honest description of their personality and needs.

## How does adoption work?

Each shelter has its own procedure, but the steps are similar.

### 1. First contact

You will often fill in a questionnaire: household, home, garden, working hours, experience with dogs, other pets. It is not a test: your answers help the team point you towards the right dog.

### 2. Meeting the dog

You meet the dog, sometimes several times, and go for a first walk together. If you already have a dog, a meeting on neutral ground is often arranged. Take your time: better to come back a second time than to decide on impulse.

### 3. Questions to ask

- How long has it been at the shelter, and why was it given up or found?
- How does it behave with children, other dogs and cats?
- Can it be left alone? How is it in the car or in town?
- Does it have any known health problems or ongoing treatment?
- What has it already learned (walking on a lead, house-training)?

### 4. Paperwork

On adoption day, you sign a contract and pay the adoption fee. You receive the dog’s European pet passport, and the shelter **transfers the DogID registration** to your name. If you live in Wallonia, you must show the **extract from the central register** (the *permis de détention*), requested from your municipality and valid for 30 days. If you live in Flanders, Brussels or abroad, no Walloon document is needed, even at a Walloon shelter.

## How much does adoption cost?

Adoption fees vary from shelter to shelter, and sometimes with the dog’s age or size. They are not the “price” of the dog: they cover part of the care it has already received (identification, vaccines, worming, neutering, vet care) and the running of the shelter, which relies heavily on donations and volunteers. Compared with the cost of that care paid separately, it is usually very reasonable.

## The first weeks at home

A shelter dog has been through big changes. It needs time to understand its new world. Many specialists talk about the “3-3-3 rule”: roughly three days to decompress, three weeks to learn the routine, three months to feel truly at home. Every dog has its own pace.

### The first few days

- Keep things calm: no big welcome party or visits from the whole family.
- Give it a corner of its own where nobody disturbs it.
- Set up a simple routine: meals, walks and rest at regular times.
- Keep walks short, always on a lead, in quiet places. A newly arrived dog does not yet know its name or your home: it can run off very easily.

### What may happen

It is common for an adopted dog to eat little in the first few days, have toilet accidents, be very clingy or, on the contrary, rather distant. These reactions usually fade with time. If you notice intense fear, aggression or separation anxiety that does not improve, do not wait: talk to the shelter, your vet, a dog trainer or a behaviourist.

> **Good to know**: book a vet appointment in the first few weeks, even if your dog seems perfectly healthy. It is a chance to get to know each other, check the chip and vaccines and ask your questions.

### Building the bond

The bond grows through positive shared moments: play, walks, short training sessions with rewards, cuddles when it asks for them. Respect its signals: a dog that turns its head away, licks its lips or moves away is telling you it needs space.

## What if it does not work out?

Despite everyone’s best efforts, an adoption sometimes does not work. Do not struggle alone: most shelters offer support and, if necessary, will take the dog back rather than see it sold on or abandoned. Never try to rehome it yourself through a classified ad without talking to the shelter first.

## The adopter’s checklist

- I have chosen a licensed shelter and looked at the dogs’ profiles.
- I have met the dog at least once, ideally twice.
- I have asked about its personality, health and background.
- If I live in Wallonia, I have requested my extract from the municipality.
- The DogID transfer is in my name and I have its passport.
- Its corner, its kit and a calm first few weeks are organised.
- I know which vet and, if needed, which dog trainer to contact nearby.
