Vaccines, wormers, spot-ons, tablets… Preventive care can feel complicated when you first get a dog. Yet it rests on a few simple principles, and it protects your companion from serious, sometimes fatal diseases. This guide gives you the key reference points for Belgium. Your dog’s exact schedule is always decided with your vet, based on its age, health and lifestyle.

## Why vaccinate your dog?

A vaccine teaches the immune system to recognise a virus or bacterium before the dog is actually exposed to it. Vaccinating your dog protects it, and also limits the spread of disease among other dogs in your neighbourhood, at the boarding kennel or in the park. Some diseases, such as parvovirus, are still common in unprotected puppies and can be fatal within days.

## Core vaccines

Vets talk about “core” vaccines, recommended for every dog wherever it lives:

- **Canine distemper**: a serious viral disease affecting the respiratory, digestive and nervous systems.
- **Infectious canine hepatitis**: affects the liver in particular.
- **Parvovirus**: a highly contagious haemorrhagic gastroenteritis, especially dangerous for puppies.

Most dogs are also vaccinated against **leptospirosis**, a bacterial disease spread through rodent urine and stagnant water. With Belgium’s ponds, ditches and puddles, it is widely recommended. Leptospirosis can also infect humans.

### The puppy schedule

A puppy first receives protection through its mother’s milk, but this fades over the weeks. That is why it gets several injections close together, usually starting at 6 to 8 weeks, then every 3 to 4 weeks until about 16 weeks. A booster follows at around one year of age.

### Adult boosters

After that, the rhythm depends on the vaccine: some components are given every year (such as leptospirosis), others every three years depending on the product used. Your vet records everything in your dog’s European pet passport and tells you when the next booster is due.

> **Good to know**: the annual vaccination visit is also a proper health check: weight, teeth, heart, skin and joints. It is often where a problem is spotted early.

## Lifestyle vaccines

### Kennel cough

This term covers several infectious agents that cause a dry, hacking cough. It spreads easily wherever dogs mix. Most **boarding kennels, daycare centres and puppy classes** ask for this vaccine, which is often given up the nose. Think about it a few weeks before a kennel stay.

### Rabies

Belgium is officially rabies-free, so vaccination is no longer compulsory for a dog that stays in the country. It is, however, **compulsory for travel**: to cross a border with your dog, it must be microchipped, have a European pet passport and a valid rabies vaccination. The first vaccine can be given from 12 weeks and only becomes valid 21 days later, so a puppy cannot travel before 15 weeks.

### Other vaccines

Depending on where you travel or how your dog lives (hunting, trips to southern Europe), your vet may suggest extra protection, for example against leishmaniasis or babesiosis. Tell them about your holiday plans.

## Worming: protection against internal parasites

Intestinal worms (roundworms, hookworms, tapeworms…) are very common, especially in puppies, which can be infected from birth. They can cause diarrhoea, weight loss and a swollen belly, and some can be passed on to people, particularly young children.

### A schedule that changes with age

- **Puppies**: wormed often, usually every two weeks until about 12 weeks, then monthly until 6 months. The breeder or shelter will have started: make a note of what has been done.
- **Adults**: the frequency depends on the risk. A dog that hunts, eats prey, lives with young children or meets lots of other dogs will be treated more often than one living in a flat. Two to four times a year is common, but some dogs need monthly treatment.

Rather than worming “just in case”, your vet may also suggest a faecal test to tailor the treatment.

> **Good to know**: always picking up after your dog is not just good manners. It is one of the best ways to limit the spread of parasites in parks and gardens.

## Fleas and ticks: external parasites

### Fleas

A single visible flea often means a whole population has settled in your home (eggs and larvae in carpets, beds and sofas). Fleas cause itching, sometimes allergies, and transmit a tapeworm. Treat the dog and, if necessary, the home.

### Ticks

In Belgium, ticks are active for much of the year, especially from spring to autumn, in woods, long grass and even gardens. They can transmit diseases such as Lyme disease, anaplasmosis and babesiosis. After every walk in nature, check your dog (ears, neck, armpits, between the toes) and remove ticks with a tick hook, without squeezing them.

### Which product?

Spot-ons, tablets, collars: there are many options, with different durations and spectrums of action. Some are prescription-only. Ask your vet rather than picking off the shelf, and **never** use a dog product on a cat: some ingredients are highly toxic to them.

## Building prevention into everyday life

- Note the dates of vaccines, wormers and flea and tick treatments in a diary or in the health record of your Doggys profile.
- Keep the European pet passport somewhere safe and take it to every vet visit.
- Plan ahead for kennel stays and trips abroad: some vaccines must be given several weeks in advance.
- Tell your vet about any reaction after a vaccine (marked tiredness, swelling, vomiting). Serious reactions are rare, but they should always be taken seriously.

## In short

Core vaccines for every dog, lifestyle vaccines where needed, worming and parasite protection matched to the risk: prevention is tailor-made. An annual visit to your vet is the best time to review everything and adjust your dog’s programme at each stage of life.
