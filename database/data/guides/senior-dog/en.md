The muzzle is turning grey, there is more napping, it takes a little longer to get up in the morning… Your dog is entering its senior years. This is often when the bond between you is at its deepest. With a few adjustments and attentive care, an older dog can stay happy, curious and comfortable for a long time. Here is how to support them.

## When is a dog a senior?

There is no single age: it all depends on size.

- **Small breeds** (under 10 kg): often around 9 to 10 years.
- **Medium breeds**: around 8 years.
- **Large and giant breeds**: sometimes as early as 6 or 7 years.

Ageing is also very individual. Rather than age alone, it is the changes you notice that matter.

## Changes to watch for

Ageing is normal, but some signs always deserve a vet’s opinion, because they may point to an illness that can be treated or eased.

### The body

- **Stiffness, limping**, difficulty climbing stairs or jumping into the car: arthritis is very common and often underestimated.
- **Weight gain or loss** without any change in diet.
- **Drinking and urinating more**, accidents indoors: report these quickly, as they can point to kidney or hormonal problems or diabetes.
- **Bad breath**, difficulty chewing: dental problems are painful and common.
- **Lumps** under the skin: most are benign, but only a vet can tell.
- **Coughing, breathlessness**, tiring quickly on exercise.

### Senses and behaviour

- **Failing sight or hearing**: it startles when touched and no longer responds when called.
- **Disorientation**: it gets lost in the house, stares at a wall, waits at the hinge side of the door.
- **Sleep problems**: sleeping during the day, restless or vocal at night.
- **Personality changes**: irritability, anxiety, a greater need for company.

These last signs may point to **canine cognitive dysfunction**, the canine equivalent of cognitive decline. There are ways to slow it down and improve comfort: talk to your vet.

> **Good to know**: a dog that is “slowing down” is not always just old. It is often in pain. Chronic pain shows quietly in dogs: less interest in play, licking a joint, avoiding certain positions. The right treatment can give them back real quality of life.

## Veterinary care

For senior dogs, most vets recommend a **check-up every six months** rather than once a year. It may include:

- a full clinical examination (heart, joints, teeth, eyes, skin, palpation);
- monitoring of weight and body condition;
- depending on the case, blood and urine tests, a blood pressure reading or imaging.

These check-ups help detect age-related diseases early and adjust treatment. Keep up with prevention too: vaccinations as advised by your vet, worming and flea and tick protection.

## Adapting the diet

An older dog often needs less energy, but still needs good-quality protein to maintain muscle mass. Your vet may suggest:

- a **senior food**, or one adapted to a particular condition (kidneys, joints, heart);
- **smaller, more frequent meals**, which are easier to digest;
- **wet food** or softened kibble if teeth are a problem;
- possibly supplements, for example for the joints, only on advice.

Keep an eye on how much it drinks: fresh water must always be within reach, in several places if moving around is difficult.

## Keeping active, gently

Movement remains essential for muscles, joints, digestion and mood.

- **Shorter but more frequent walks**, at their pace, on soft ground where possible.
- **Let them sniff**: sniffing is tiring and deeply satisfying for a dog, even an old one.
- **Brain games**: snuffle mats, treat toys, small training exercises. You can absolutely teach an old dog new tricks!
- **Avoid intense exertion**, jumping and hot weather.

On veterinary advice, **physiotherapy**, **hydrotherapy** or **osteopathy** can help a dog with arthritis stay mobile and in less pain.

## Adapting your home

A few simple changes make daily life much easier:

- **An orthopaedic bed**: thick, away from draughts and easy to get into.
- **Non-slip rugs** on tiles and wooden floors: slipping is common and frightening.
- **A ramp or step** for the car or sofa, if allowed.
- **Raised bowls** for large dogs that struggle to bend down.
- **A gate** at the top of the stairs if their sight is failing.
- **Stable landmarks**: avoid moving furniture if sight or memory is declining.

Grooming also needs more attention: gentler brushing, more frequent nail trims (nails wear down less), cleaning eyes and ears. A **mobile groomer** spares them the stress of the journey and waiting at a salon.

## Nurturing the bond

An older dog needs routine, calm and company. Respect their naps, let them know you are approaching if their hearing is poor (a vibration on the floor, a gesture in their line of sight), and keep sharing small moments together. If you bring a new puppy home, protect your senior’s peace and quiet.

## When the end draws near

One day, the question of quality of life will arise. Your vet can help you assess it with concrete criteria: appetite, mobility, pain, continence, interest in the family, good days and bad days. There is no “easy” right decision, but there is a decision made with love and guided by a professional. Feel free to talk about it early, so you do not have to decide in a rush.

> **Good to know**: after your dog dies, remember to report it in DogID. Your vet can help with this, and with the choice between cremation and burial.

## In short

More frequent vet visits, an adapted diet, gentle exercise, a safe home and plenty of affection: a senior dog needs small adjustments to make the most of its golden years. Watch them, listen to their signals and never hesitate to ask for advice.
