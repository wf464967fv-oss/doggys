A puppy padding around the house is a promise of endless cuddles… and a commitment of ten to fifteen years, sometimes more. Before you fall for a little ball of fluff, take the time to check that the timing, the home and the dog are right. This checklist helps you ask the right questions, without any guilt: with good preparation, adopting a dog is almost always a lovely story.

## Are you ready? Questions to ask as a family

Getting a dog is a family decision. Gather everyone who lives in the house and answer these questions honestly:

- **Time**: a puppy cannot be left alone for eight hours at a stretch. During the first months it needs to go out every two to three hours, to play, to learn and to sleep a lot. Who will look after it during the day?
- **Energy**: walks in all weathers, daily training, sometimes short nights. Are you up for several outings a day, even on a wet November evening?
- **Stability**: is a house move, a new baby or a job change on the horizon? It is not a deal-breaker, but it is better to plan ahead.
- **Allergies**: is anyone in the household allergic? Spending a few hours with dogs before deciding avoids heartbreak later.
- **Everyone on board**: does everyone want this dog, including the person who will actually do most of the walks?

> **Good to know**: if you rent, check your lease and any building rules, then talk to your landlord before the puppy arrives. A written agreement avoids a lot of tension later on.

## What a dog really costs

The purchase price or adoption fee is only a small part of the total cost. As a rough guide, budget for:

- **Start-up costs**: identification and registration (often already done by the breeder or shelter), first vaccinations, worming, a bed, lead, harness, bowls, toys, and possibly a crate and baby gates.
- **Monthly costs**: good-quality food, flea and tick treatments, treats and replacement toys. These vary a lot depending on the size of the dog.
- **Yearly costs**: booster vaccinations, a check-up at the vet, grooming if needed, pet insurance, and a dog tax in some Belgian municipalities.
- **The unexpected**: an upset stomach, an injured paw or a swallowed object can easily cost several hundred euros. Setting a little aside each month, or taking out insurance, means you never have to choose between care and your budget.
- **Time away**: boarding kennels, pet sitters or dog walkers during holidays or long working days.

## Choosing the right dog for your life

A crush on a photo is rarely the best guide. Think about your daily routine instead.

### Size, energy and needs

A border collie in a flat with no daily activity will soon be bored and may develop unwanted habits. A bulldog will struggle on long summer hikes. Find out what the breed was originally bred for (guarding, herding, hunting, companionship): it tells you a lot about its needs. A mixed-breed dog can also make a wonderful companion.

### Coat and grooming

Some dogs shed a lot, others need regular professional grooming (poodles, bichons, schnauzers…). Factor that time and cost into your decision.

### Puppy or adult?

A puppy demands an enormous amount of time and training. An adult dog, for instance from a shelter, often has a known personality and may already be house-trained. Both are wonderful choices; what matters is that they match how much time you have.

## Licensed breeder or shelter: where to adopt?

In Belgium, puppy sales are regulated by the three regions. Whether you go to a breeder or a shelter, always choose a **licensed** one:

- **Licensed breeder**: in Wallonia every dog breeder needs a licence number, which must appear in adverts. In Flanders, breeders with three or more litters a year have an **HK number**, and every advert must show the puppy’s **microchip number**. A good breeder lets you meet the mother and see where the puppies live, and is happy to answer all your questions.
- **Licensed shelter**: dozens of dogs of all ages are waiting for a family. The team knows their personalities and will help you find the right match.

Steer clear of adverts that look too good to be true: “pedigree” puppies at bargain prices, handovers in a car park, a seller in a hurry, no visit possible. These are classic signs of puppy trafficking, often involving sick and poorly socialised animals.

## Paperwork and legal requirements in Belgium

Some rules apply throughout Belgium, others depend on your region.

- **Identification and registration**: every dog must be microchipped by a vet and registered in the **DogID** database before it is 8 weeks old, and in any case before it changes hands (sale or gift). Make sure the registration is transferred to your name.
- **Minimum age**: a puppy may not be sold or given away before 8 weeks.
- **Passport**: an identified dog receives a European pet passport, in which the vet records vaccinations among other things.
- **In Wallonia**: anyone acquiring an animal (buying, adopting or receiving one) must show an **extract from the central register** of environmental and animal welfare offences, known as the “permis de détention”. You request it from your municipality and it is valid for 30 days. A digital certificate is due to replace it: check the current procedure with your municipality.
- **In Flanders and Brussels**: no prior permit, but the same rules on identification, registration and buying from licensed breeders or shelters apply.

> **Good to know**: always ask for the documents when you collect your puppy: passport, DogID registration certificate, vaccination record and a sale or adoption contract. A responsible seller will have them ready without being asked.

## The final checklist before you say yes

Print it out or keep it on your phone:

1. The whole family agrees and knows who does what (meals, walks, training).
2. The monthly budget and a reserve for emergencies are planned.
3. Your home is suitable (and your lease allows it).
4. The breed or type of dog fits your lifestyle.
5. You have found a licensed breeder or shelter and visited in person.
6. You have met the mother (for a puppy from a breeder) and asked about health and socialisation.
7. In Wallonia, you have requested your extract from the municipality.
8. You have chosen a vet for a first check-up, ideally in the week after arrival.
9. You have found a puppy class or dog trainer nearby.
10. You have a plan for holidays and absences over the coming year.

If you can tick most of these boxes, you are on the right track. If a few are missing, that is not a failure: waiting a few months to adopt in the right conditions is often the best gift you can give your future companion.
